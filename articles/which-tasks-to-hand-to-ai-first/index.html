Which tasks should you hand to AI first? Four tests

Most AI pilots fail, and it's rarely the technology. Four quick tests to pick a first task that pays off, plus the tasks we'd leave alone.

By the Lighter Hours team·October 8, 2026·3 min read

Maybe you've already tried. You signed up for the AI tool everyone's talking about, got excited for a week, and then it quietly stopped being used. It felt like the problem was you.

It wasn't. You're in very large company.

Most AI projects don't make it

  • MIT's "GenAI Divide" report found about 95% of generative AI pilots at companies show little to no measurable financial impact. The best returns came from back-office work, not flashy projects.
  • Gartner predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after the trial stage by the end of 2025.
  • BCG found 74% of companies have yet to show real value from their use of AI.

The pattern behind the failures is usually the same. They started with the biggest, most exciting idea instead of the right one.

Start by asking the people doing the work

When we help a company bring in AI, we don't start with our own ideas. We start by asking the people stuck doing the work every day: what would you love to take off your plate? The best ideas never come from a leadership slide.

Then score every idea on four tests

  • It happens often. Daily or weekly, not once a quarter. Automating something you do twice a year never pays back.
  • It's text in and text out. Emails, tickets, documents, reports, data. AI is strong here. It can't restock a shelf.
  • It follows rules. If a new hire could do it with a good checklist, AI probably can too.
  • A mistake is cheap to catch. A person can review the result before it matters. If one wrong answer could cost you a customer, it's not a first project.

An idea that passes all four is a great first project. Three out of four can work with a person reviewing each result. Two or fewer: park it.

One team, one owner, then prove it

Don't launch to everyone. Pick one team, name one person who owns it, and start with AI tools you already pay for. Measure one number before you start, so you can prove it worked. Then move on to the next one.

Looking back at what we've built, the tools that struggled each failed at least one of these tests.

Tasks we'd leave alone, for now

  • Anything physical.
  • Decisions about people: hiring, firing, discipline.
  • Anything where one wrong answer is expensive and nobody would catch it in time.
  • Rare tasks. Automation needs volume to pay off.

Try it on your own list

Write down five tasks your team complains about. Score each one against the four tests. Start with the winner.
