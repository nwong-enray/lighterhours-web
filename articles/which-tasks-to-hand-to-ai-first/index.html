Which tasks should you hand to AI first? Four tests

Most AI projects stall because they start with the wrong task. Four quick tests to pick one that pays off, plus the tasks I'd leave alone.

October 8, 2026

When a company decides to "use AI," the first idea is usually the biggest one: automate sales, replace the call center, predict everything. Those projects stall. The ones that work start small and a little boring.

When I was asked to help roll AI out across a company, I didn't start with my own ideas. I started by asking the people doing the work.

Step one: ask the people doing the work

A short form went out to staff: what busywork would you love to take off your plate? The best ideas come from the people stuck doing a task every day, not from a leadership slide.

Step two: score every idea on four tests

  • It happens often. Daily or weekly, not once a quarter. Automating something you do twice a year never pays back.
  • It's text in and text out. Emails, tickets, documents, reports, data. AI is strong here. It can't restock a shelf.
  • It follows rules. If a new hire could do it with a good checklist, AI probably can too.
  • A mistake is cheap to catch. A person can review the output before it matters. If one wrong answer could cost you a customer, it's not a first project.

An idea that passes all four is a great first project. Three out of four can work with a person reviewing each result. Two or fewer: park it.

Step three: one team, one champion

Don't launch to everyone. Pick one team, name one person who owns it, and start with the AI tools you already pay for. Most office suites now include some. Build something custom only where it clearly pays off.

Step four: prove it, then scale

Measure one number before you start. Test on real cases the AI hasn't seen. Hand the working tool to the team with simple instructions, then move on to the next one.

To be honest about where I am: this is the method I'm using right now, and the company-wide rollout is still early. But looking back at the tools I've already built, the ones that struggled each failed at least one of these tests.

Tasks I'd leave alone, for now

  • Anything physical.
  • Decisions about people: hiring, firing, discipline.
  • Anything where one wrong answer is expensive and nobody would catch it in time.
  • Rare tasks. Automation needs volume to pay off.

Try it on your own list

Write down five tasks your team complains about. Score each one against the four tests. Start with the winner.
