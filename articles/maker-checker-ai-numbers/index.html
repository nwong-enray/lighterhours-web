Before AI touches your numbers, give it a checker

AI-written numbers look perfect, which is exactly the danger. Real cases where it went wrong, and the simple rule that keeps it out of your meetings.

By the Lighter Hours team·October 8, 2026·3 min read

Picture the moment. You're presenting numbers to your partners, your bank, or your best client. Someone asks a simple question about one figure, and you realize the AI-built report was wrong. Not obviously wrong. Confidently, beautifully, wrong.

That's the danger with AI and numbers. The formatting is perfect. The mistakes don't look like mistakes.

It happens to serious people

  • Two New York lawyers were fined $5,000 after filing a brief with six court cases ChatGPT had made up.
  • Deloitte agreed to partially refund the Australian government for a report that included a made-up court quote and references to research that doesn't exist.
  • Stanford researchers found leading AI models gave wrong answers 69% to 88% of the time on specific legal questions.

None of these were careless people. They trusted output that looked finished.

The feed we had to turn off

We learned this ourselves. Early on, we set up a daily summary of AI-written insights about how the business was doing. Every morning, a few sentences on what changed and why. It sounded great.

Then it started raising alarms about problems that didn't exist. Nothing broke, but people started double-checking everything it said, which defeated the purpose. We paused it. The lesson stuck: an AI conclusion needs a second check before anyone sees it.

The maker-checker rule

Banks have done this forever. The person who prepares a payment isn't the person who approves it. We apply the same rule to AI: whatever builds the numbers never gets the final say on whether they're right. An independent check confirms them first, and nothing reaches leadership until it passes.

What it caught

The first real audit is why we trust this setup. The check caught records pointing to the wrong place and entries marked inactive by mistake, before the report ever reached leadership. Once those were fixed, the fees matched to the penny.

None of those errors came from the AI being dumb. They were the kind of quiet mismatch a tired person makes at 6 PM too. The difference is that now something catches them every time.

How to start in your business

  • Pick one report that already goes out regularly and that people trust a little too much.
  • Make sure the AI can read your data but never change it.
  • Add the check before you add speed. Speed without a check just moves wrong numbers faster.
  • Keep a person signing off until the check has a few clean months behind it.

The goal isn't to replace the person who knows the numbers. It's to make sure that person never has to say "the AI got it wrong" in a meeting.
