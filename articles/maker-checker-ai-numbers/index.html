Before AI touches your numbers, give it a checker

AI is fast with reports and wrong just often enough to hurt. The maker-checker rule I use to catch mistakes before leadership ever sees them.

October 8, 2026

AI is very good at turning raw data into a clean report. It's also wrong just often enough to be dangerous, and it's never wrong in an obvious way. The numbers look right. The formatting is perfect. That's the problem.

I learned this the hard way.

The feed I had to turn off

Early on, I set up a daily feed of AI-written insights about how the business was doing. It sounded great: every morning, a few sentences on what changed and why.

Then it started raising alarms about problems that didn't exist. Nothing broke, but people started double-checking everything it said, which defeated the purpose. I paused it. The lesson stuck: an AI conclusion needs a second check before anyone sees it.

The maker-checker rule

Banks have done this forever. The person who prepares a payment isn't the person who approves it. I apply the same rule to AI.

  • The maker is one AI that builds the numbers: the report, the payout file, the fee calculation.
  • The checker is a separate AI with read-only access. It doesn't look at the maker's work and nod along. It writes its own queries from scratch and reports pass or fail.
  • On top of that, IDs get checked against names, so a location number really points to the place you think it does, and totals get tied to a second source.

Nothing leaves the team until the checker passes it.

What it actually caught

The first real audit is why I trust this setup. The checker caught wrong location IDs and some records marked inactive by mistake, before the report ever reached leadership. Once those were fixed, fees matched to the penny.

None of those errors came from the AI being dumb. They were the kind of quiet mismatch a tired person makes at 6 PM too. The difference is that now something catches them every time.

Cross-checking analysis, not just arithmetic

The same idea works for bigger questions. When I needed to explain a business trend to leadership, one AI model did the analysis and two other models challenged its conclusions over three rounds. Then a person, me, corrected the assumptions that were off before anything went out.

Two details made this safe and cheap:

  • The cross-checking models only saw percentages, never dollar figures.
  • The whole exercise cost under $0.50 in AI fees and took two days instead of weeks.

How to start this in your business

  • Pick one report that already goes out regularly and that people already trust a little too much.
  • Give the AI read-only access to the data. It should never be able to change the source.
  • Add the checker before you add speed. Speed without a checker just moves wrong numbers faster.
  • Keep a person signing off until the checker has a few clean months behind it.

The goal isn't to replace the person who knows the numbers. It's to make sure that person never has to say "the AI got it wrong" in a meeting.
